HuMoTAR: Affordance-Aware Semantic Retargeting for Humanoid-Object Interaction

Narayanan Palghat Parameswaran1 Jay Karhade1 Haoyang He2 Neehar Peri3 Jay Patrikar2 Dong-Ki Kim2 Shayegan Omidshafiei2 Sebastian Scherer1,2 Deva Ramanan1
1Carnegie Mellon University 2FieldAI 3California Institute of Technology

Key Takeaways

  • With HuMoTAR, we posit that frontier agentic models can capture both the semantic intent and physical requirements of a motion for generalizable humanoid retargeting.
  • Closed-loop simulation feedback raises dynamics-aware semantic success from 17.5% (HOI-Retarget + SPIDER) to 47.5% with a single Codex agent, by letting agents iteratively refine motion and control.
  • An agentic swarm beats a single Codex agent by 20 percentage points under a fixed time budget, but compute-matched Codex surpasses it by 7.5 percentage points, at the cost of 4× the elapsed time.
  • Prior experience cuts object-tracking error from 23.35 to 7.77 cm in a shelf-righting task, with the experience stored as summaries of strategies and outcomes from related tasks, at equal compute and time.

1. Semantic humanoid retargeting

Semantic humanoid retargeting preserves the intended task rather than the precise movements a human uses to perform it. When a person carries and places a chair, what matters is what happens to the chair and where it ends up, not how the person holds it or how the body exactly moves. We therefore define the task semantically, and leave the robot’s contact strategy, posture and controller free to change.

The first gap is kinematic: current retargeting methods preserve the movement, not the task. A humanoid differs from a human in proportions, joint limits and actuation, so each demonstration must first be adapted to its body. Most human-to-humanoid methods formulate this as a kinematic problem1,2: they fit the human’s motion to the robot, matching its body poses and object-relative contacts1. This reproduces how the human moved, even when the robot’s body calls for a different strategy.

The second gap is dynamic: a kinematically feasible reference is not necessarily dynamically feasible. Sampling-based physics methods address this by posing retargeting as a constrained optimization over the robot’s motor commands, sampling many candidate command sequences in a physics simulator and keeping those whose simulated motion stays closest to the reference3. However, errors in the input motion reference can lead them to the wrong contact strategy, and their open-loop execution is sensitive to the robot’s starting pose.

We posit that frontier agentic models can capture both the semantic intent and physical requirements of a motion. We test this with HuMoTAR, an agentic approach to semantic humanoid retargeting. Through closed-loop simulation feedback, the agents write, test and iteratively revise both the motion reference, addressing the kinematic gap, and the control code, addressing the dynamic gap.

2. HuMoTAR: semantic retargeting as iterative code refinement

HuMoTAR uses an agentic framework that writes, tests and revises motion and control code, starting from a human demonstration and an initial kinematic reference. The initial kinematic reference can come from any retargeting method; in our experiments, we use HOI-Retarget1. The agent also receives the task scene and the target object trajectory, scaled to the robot’s size. The output is a reference motion for the whole body and hands.

Each revision is tested in simulation. The agent executes the reference on a Unitree G1 humanoid with Dex3 hands11 in MuJoCo7, with SONIC4 as the whole-body controller and manipulation control code that it writes itself. After each simulation, the agent inspects images of the execution, object-tracking errors and contact measurements, and uses this feedback to adjust grasp placement, body posture and the timing of lifting or release.

2.1 Study design

We ask three questions: how much does closed-loop simulation feedback help, do agentic swarms outperform a single agent, and can agents reuse what they learned on related tasks? We answer the first two on 40 tasks, with four configurations and two budget settings, and explore the third in Section 3.3.

We study three HuMoTAR configurations against two baselines, one classical and one open-loop agentic. The HuMoTAR configurations run GPT-6 Astra9 inside Codex10 agents:

  • A1 · Single Codex agent tests and revises its solution in a persistent coding session.
  • A2 · Independent agentic swarm runs four Codex agents that develop their solutions independently.
  • A3 · Collaborative agentic swarm runs four Codex agents that can communicate directly with one another to share findings, request help and critique strategies.

The classical baseline (B0) optimizes the robot’s motion directly; the open-loop agentic baseline (B1) uses the same model as HuMoTAR without its closed refinement loop:

  • B0 · HOI-Retarget + SPIDER (classical) executes HOI-Retarget1 references with SPIDER3, which we adapt to G1 with Dex3.
  • B1 · Astra one-shot (open-loop agentic) generates motion and control code in one response, without tool calls or further revisions.

We compare configurations under a fixed time budget and under matched compute, to study the compute–time tradeoff between agentic swarms and longer single-agent iterative refinement. With a fixed 30-minute budget, each swarm uses four times the agent-hours of a single agent. At matched compute, the collaborative swarm runs for 30 minutes and a single agent for 120 minutes, so both use two agent-hours.

2.2 Tasks and metrics

Our metrics score the task, not the robot’s movement. We evaluate 20 HUMOTO5 and 20 OMOMO6 tasks. Every execution runs in physics simulation, so the metrics are dynamics-aware: a motion scores well only if the robot can physically carry it out. Object tracking compares the simulated object motion with the demonstrated trajectory scaled for G1. We do not require the object to reach each position at the same time in the human demonstration and the robot execution, since the robot may move at a different speed. Instead, we compare corresponding stages of the object’s motion, and average object-tracking errors equally across tasks.

  • Mean ADD (cm) ↓: The average distance between corresponding points on the object at aligned poses. It captures both position and orientation error.
  • Position error (cm) ↓: The average distance between the simulated and target object positions.
  • Orientation error (°) ↓: The average angular difference between the simulated and target object orientations.
  • Semantic success ↑: Astra compares the human demonstration video with the executed robot video and judges whether the robot completes the intended task. Each task receives a PASS or FAIL judgment. Across tasks, we report the percentage that pass. The same model drives the agents and the judge.

3.1 How much does closed-loop simulation feedback help?

The gain comes from iterative refinement, not from the model alone: Astra one-shot (B1) does worse than HOI-Retarget + SPIDER (B0), while HuMoTAR’s single Codex agent (A1) reaches 47.5%. We compare the single Codex agent (A1) with HOI-Retarget + SPIDER (B0) on all 40 tasks, and include Astra one-shot (B1) to measure performance without iterative refinement.

Where does this gain come from? Simulation feedback drives revisions across the whole interaction, from the starting pose to placement and release. Each example below shows what the agent changed, the feedback that prompted the change and its effect on execution.

To see how these revisions add up, we follow one refinement from start to finish: the agent selected from HuMoTAR’s collaborative swarm (A3) on the clothes-stand task from OMOMO6, in which the robot must lift the stand, carry it through a turn and place it on the floor. Its five saved trials show the refinement proceeding in stages. The agent first establishes a grasp, then improves the carry and finally completes placement, with one unsuccessful controller revision along the way. 3.2 Do agentic swarms outperform a single agent?

It depends. Whether a swarm beats a single agent depends on which budget is fixed. When time is fixed, HuMoTAR’s swarms win; when compute is fixed, its single Codex agent (A1) wins by running longer. The choice comes down to what matters more to you: a good solution quickly, or the best solution for a given amount of compute.

Start with a fixed budget of 30 minutes, where we compare HuMoTAR’s single Codex agent (A1) with its independent swarm (A2) and collaborative swarm (A3). Each swarm runs four agents, so it uses four times the agent-hours.

Here, running more agents helps, and communication between them helps most. Four independent agents can reduce “seed luck” and improve reliability, since one agent may find a solution that another misses. The independent swarm (A2) improves object tracking and achieves semantic success on 21 tasks, compared with 19 for the single Codex agent (A1); on 40 tasks, this difference is small.

Letting the agents talk to each other matters far more: with the same four-agent budget, semantic success rises from 52.5% for the independent swarm (A2) to 67.5% for the collaborative swarm (A3). The agents divide experiments, test and improve each other’s controllers, and share unsuccessful results so other agents know what has already been tried. They also use feedback from other agents to correct mistakes in their evaluation. In a monitor task where the robot must lift and move a monitor, one agent identifies an object drop near the end of a rollout that another had judged successful. The second agent acknowledges the missed failure and identifies controlled placement as the next step.

Independent agents, on the other hand, sometimes spend part of their budget on overlapping approaches, without access to each other’s findings.

The picture changes when compute, rather than time, is held fixed. We compare HuMoTAR’s collaborative swarm (A3), with four Codex agents running for 30 minutes each, against the single Codex agent (A1) running for 120 minutes. Both settings use two agent-hours.

Given four times as long, the single Codex agent (A1) reaches 75% semantic success, compared with 67.5% for the collaborative swarm (A3). A swarm is therefore the better choice when a solution is needed quickly, but a longer single-agent run will win when time is not of the essence.

3.3 Can agents reuse what they learned on related tasks?

By experience, we mean written summaries of what the agents learned on other tasks: the strategies they tested, what worked and what failed. Given these summaries while refining a new task, the agent need not rediscover what already worked or failed.

We test this on a shelf-righting task, in which the robot raises a shelf from its side to an upright position. The experience comes from four related furniture interactions: carrying a side table, lifting a dining chair, walking with a low chair, and pulling and pushing a dining chair. With this experience, object-tracking error falls to 7.77 cm, compared with 23.35 cm without it. This is a single example rather than a general result.

We first run HuMoTAR’s collaborative swarm (A3) for 15 minutes and select the best valid motion. From this shared starting solution, we run two 15-minute continuations, one with the related experience and one without, with equal time and compute. This isolates what the experience changes during refinement.

4. Discussion, limitations and future work

4.1 Findings and failure cases

The difference is closed loop versus open loop. Run open loop, Astra one-shot (B1) does worse than HOI-Retarget + SPIDER (B0). Closing the loop with simulation feedback lets the same model iteratively refine motion and control, raising dynamics-aware semantic success to 47.5% with a single Codex agent (A1). How best to spend the agents’ budget then depends on what is scarce. The collaborative swarm (A3) reaches good solutions fastest, while the single Codex agent (A1), given the same compute over a longer run, reaches better ones. We encourage the community to study this compute–time tradeoff across more interactions, and to investigate how communication and shared experience make better use of a limited refinement budget.

Failure cases. Not every interaction is within reach, and some fail because of the robot’s hands rather than its motion. In the example below, G1 must lift and swirl a plate. It lifts the plate but does not complete the swirling motion. The agents adjust finger placement and try two-handed support, yet the grasp remains unstable during rotation: the fingers of G1’s Dex3 hands struggle to hold the plate’s thin rim. The tested motion and controller revisions do not compensate for this morphological gap.

4.2 Limitations and future work

Our study is small in scale. We evaluate 40 tasks in simulation: every execution runs in MuJoCo with SONIC as the whole-body controller, and every initial reference comes from HOI-Retarget, so whether HuMoTAR improves references from other kinematic retargeters remains untested. Our results are also bounded by the simulator itself: MuJoCo approximates contact, friction and object dynamics, and the agents optimize against those approximations, so a motion that succeeds in simulation may still fail on hardware. This study does not address the sim-to-real gap. At this scale, small differences, such as 19 versus 21 successful tasks, may reflect run-to-run variation, and the experience result rests on a single task.

These limitations point to future work. HuMoTAR’s references are a starting point for deployable policies: our experiments use SONIC without task-specific policy training. A next step is to evaluate whether RL fine-tuning on these references helps the robot handle disturbances and changes in the scene, and to deploy the resulting policies on hardware.

Our results point to five further directions:

  • Scaling refinement. How semantic success changes with the number of agents and the length of refinement, and how far the compute–time tradeoff holds across more tasks and kinematic retargeters.
  • Accumulating experience. A growing library of reports that agents can search, along with a broader test of when related experience helps and when it does not.
  • Retargeting from language. Appendix A1 shows that a text description can replace the motion reference, suggesting retargeting when no demonstration exists.
  • Hand-aware strategies. The plate example suggests that some interactions need grasps designed for the robot’s hands rather than adapted from the human’s.
  • Human evaluation. Semantic success is judged by the same model that drives the agents. A user study in which people rank the generated motions would give an independent measure of whether the robot preserves the intent of each demonstration.

Appendix

A1. Can a text description replace a motion reference?

We give a single Codex agent a detailed task description, the scene and an initial robot–object state, without a human motion reference or target object trajectory. The agent has 30 minutes to refine an execution that follows the description, using SONIC for the legs and waist and custom arm and hand control. These five examples show the supplied commands and the resulting simulated motions.

Lift and inspect a basket

Text command

A basket rests on the floor in front of the person. Bend toward it and reach down with the right hand to take its handle. Lift the basket clear of the floor while straightening the body. Bring it in front of the torso, then use the other hand to help steady and turn it for inspection. Tilt and rotate the basket so its opening and different sides can be examined, rather than simply holding it motionless. Keep it under control throughout the lift and the changes in orientation. Finish standing with the basket held in front of the body; the demonstrated sequence ends while it is still being held.

Lift, lower and lift a side table

Text command

A small wooden side table with a raised curved rail stands on the floor in front of the person. Bend down, grasp the raised rail and lift the table off the floor as the body straightens. Keep the tabletop approximately level and the legs directed downward. Lower it back onto its legs in front of the body, then pause with the table supported on the floor. Bend toward it again and repeat the lifting action. Make the separate pickup, lowering, supported pause and renewed pickup recognizable, rather than replacing them with a single static hold or dragging the table along the floor. Remain near the table and keep each movement controlled.

Walk and turn while carrying a side table

Text command

Hold the small wooden side table with both hands in front of the torso, keeping its legs clear of the floor. Walk while carrying it along a curved path that turns the body through several facing directions, then comes back toward the starting area. The table travels with the person and turns with the body; its legs remain generally downward and the tabletop remains approximately level, with natural tilting during the turns. Keep both hands supporting the table while stepping and changing direction. Finish the walking sequence still holding the table in front of the body. The interaction should show sustained transport and turning, not stationary lifting or sliding on the floor.

Lift, move and set down a clothes stand

Text command

Stand beside a tall freestanding clothes stand with a circular base and branches near the top. Reach to its vertical pole around waist height with one hand. Lift its base clear of the floor, then take a few steps to move the stand to a nearby position while keeping the pole mostly upright. Move the body together with the stand rather than reaching from a fixed stance. Lower the circular base onto the floor, allow the stand to settle upright, and release the pole. Finish beside the independently standing object with the hand withdrawn. The lift, short transport and deliberate placement should be clearly visible.

Rock a wooden chair and return it upright

Text command

Face a wooden chair and bend toward it, placing both hands around the sides of the seat or nearby frame. Tilt and rock the chair so its seat and back lean and one pair of legs rises while the other pair remains near the floor. Guide the chair back toward an upright orientation and bring all four legs down under control. Keep the hands engaged while the chair changes orientation, and adjust the body with the movement. Finish with the chair upright and supported on its legs. This is a controlled rocking and righting interaction, not carrying the chair away or allowing it to fall.

A3. How does refinement change the reference?

Refinement moves the reference hands toward the grasp the robot actually executes. For the clothes-stand task from the walkthrough in Section 3.1, run by HuMoTAR’s independent swarm (A2), we overlay the initial kinematic reference (purple) and the refined reference (blue) on the physical execution (solid gray). Their separation shows where the prescribed and executed poses differ at corresponding motion phases. Body posture can still differ, as the controller maintains balance.
