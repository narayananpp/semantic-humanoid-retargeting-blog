HuMoTAR: Affordance-Aware Semantic Retargeting for Humanoid-Object Interaction

Narayanan Palghat Parameswaran1 Jay Karhade1 Haoyang He2 Neehar Peri3 Jay Patrikar2 Dong-Ki Kim2 Shayegan Omidshafiei2 Sebastian Scherer1,2 Deva Ramanan1
1Carnegie Mellon University 2FieldAI 3California Institute of Technology

Key Takeaways

  • How should one retarget human-object motions for humanoids? We argue that robotics is about enacting changes in the environment, and so retargeted motions should produce the same object trajectory.
  • We use agentic models with a simulator-in-the-loop to produce dynamically-feasible motions. We analyze the behaviour of our harness and show that it is crucial to iterate over targets with a verification reward that prioritizes matching object trajectories.
  • Parallel sub-agents vs. sequential refinement. We explore tradeoffs on iterations of refinement, comparing sequential to parallel search. We find that parallel agents can find a good solution faster.
  • Experience from related tasks. Prior experience, stored as summaries of strategies and outcomes, cuts object-tracking error on a new humanoid-object task from 23.35 to 7.77 cm at equal compute and time budgets.

1. Retargeting should preserve the task, not the motion

We propose HuMoTAR, an agentic approach to semantic humanoid retargeting. HuMoTAR agents write, test and iteratively revise motion and control code through closed-loop simulation feedback. We posit that frontier agentic models provide useful priors for preserving a motion’s semantic intent while satisfying the robot’s physical constraints.

The videos above pair human demonstrations of carrying a chair and moving a clothes stand with their retargeted humanoid motions. Since a humanoid differs from a human in proportions, joint limits and actuation, each demonstration must first be adapted to the robot’s body. Existing human-to-humanoid retargeting methods largely formulate this as a kinematic problem, fitting the human’s motion to the robot while preserving relevant contacts with the environment. For example, HOI-Retarget optimizes the robot’s motion to match the demonstration’s body poses and object-relative contacts. However, this raises the question of what a humanoid should preserve from a demonstration.

We argue that retargeting should preserve the intended task rather than the precise movements a human uses to perform it. Consider a person who carries and places a chair with one hand. The goal is to carry the chair to a target location and place it there, not to reproduce the one-handed grasp. A humanoid may instead use both hands if that better suits its morphology and physical limits. The grasp and posture differ from the demonstration, but the task is unchanged. Concretely, we scale the demonstrated object trajectory to the robot’s size and use it as the target motion, leaving the robot’s contact strategy, posture and controller free to change.

A kinematically feasible reference is not necessarily dynamically feasible. SPIDER addresses this gap by posing retargeting as a constrained optimization over the robot’s motor commands. Specifically, it samples many candidate command sequences in a physics simulator and keeps those whose simulated motion stays closest to the reference. However, errors in the input reference can lead SPIDER to an incorrect contact strategy, and its open-loop execution is sensitive to the robot’s starting pose. In contrast, HuMoTAR revises both the motion reference and control code based on the resulting physical execution.

2. Motion refinement with simulation feedback

Configurations of the refinement loop

We compare HuMoTAR, our general approach to agentic semantic retargeting, against SPIDER’s execution of kinematic references generated by HOI-Retarget. Specifically, we use GPT-6 Astra to study iterative refinement with simulation feedback in three HuMoTAR configurations: a single Codex agent, an independent agentic swarm and a collaborative agentic swarm. We also evaluate Astra one-shot, which generates a solution without tool calls or iterative refinement.

We compare these configurations under both a fixed time budget and an equal compute budget measured in agent-hours. This lets us study the compute–time tradeoff between agentic swarms and longer single-agent iterative refinement.

  • Astra one-shot generates motion and control code in one response, without tool calls or further revisions.
  • Codex tests and revises its solution in a persistent coding session.
  • Independent agentic swarm runs four Codex agents that develop their solutions independently.
  • Collaborative agentic swarm runs four Codex agents that can communicate directly with one another to share findings, request help and critique strategies.

For each task, HuMoTAR takes as input the human demonstration in SMPL format, an initial kinematic reference from HOI-Retarget, the task scene and the target object trajectory. The object trajectory specifies the desired object motion, scaled to the robot’s size. The agents then write Python code to generate and refine a reference motion for the whole body and hands, which serves as the framework’s output.

We execute the motion references and control code in MuJoCo, using SONIC as the whole-body controller. After each simulation, the agent inspects images of the execution, object-tracking errors and contact measurements before revising its solution.

3. Evaluation metrics

We evaluate all methods on 20 HUMOTO and 20 OMOMO tasks using object-tracking error and semantic success. Object tracking compares the simulated object motion with the demonstrated trajectory scaled for G1. However, the robot may move at a different speed than the human, so we do not require the object to reach each position at the same time as in the human demonstration. Instead, we compare corresponding stages of the object’s motion.

  • Mean ADD (cm): The average distance between corresponding points on the object at aligned poses. It captures both position and orientation error. Lower is better.
  • Position error (cm): The average distance between the simulated and target object positions. Lower is better.
  • Orientation error (°): The average angular difference between the simulated and target object orientations. Lower is better.
  • Semantic success: Astra compares the human SMPL video with the executed robot video and judges whether the robot completes the intended task. Each task receives a PASS or FAIL judgment. Across tasks, we report the percentage that pass. Higher is better.

We average object-tracking errors equally across tasks.

5.2 At matched time, collaborative swarms outperform a single agent

We compare a single Codex agent with the independent and collaborative agentic swarms under the same 30-minute time budget. Since each swarm runs four agents, it uses four times as many agent-hours.

Agentic swarms improve retargeting within the same time budget, and inter-agent communication yields the strongest task completion. Running four independent agents can reduce “seed luck” and improve reliability, since one agent may find a solution that another misses. We find that the independent swarm improves object tracking and achieves semantic success on 21 tasks, compared with 19 for a single agent.

Inter-agent communication raises semantic success from 52.5% to 67.5% with the same four-agent budget. Specifically, the agents divide experiments, test and improve each other’s controllers, and share unsuccessful results so that others know what has already been tried. They also use feedback from other agents to correct mistakes in their own evaluations. For example, in a task where the robot must lift and move a monitor, one agent identifies an object drop near the end of a rollout that another had judged successful. The second agent acknowledges the missed failure and identifies controlled placement as the next step.

In contrast, independent agents cannot access each other’s findings and sometimes spend part of their budget on overlapping approaches. For example, in a task where the robot must lift, move and place a tripod, two independent agents separately test closely overlapping two-handed grasps, both unsuccessfully.

5.3 At matched compute, longer single-agent refinement wins

Under a fixed time budget, the collaborative swarm outperforms a single agent. However, it also uses four times the compute. At matched compute (agent-hours), is it better to give a single agent more time for reasoning and iterative refinement or to distribute the budget across a collaborative swarm? To answer this, we compare the collaborative swarm of four Codex agents running for 30 minutes each against one Codex agent running for 120 minutes. Both settings receive two agent-hours.

We find that longer single-agent refinement outperforms the collaborative swarm in semantic success, achieving 75% versus 67.5%, at the cost of 4× the elapsed time.

6. Prior experience improves refinement on new tasks

We find that providing related experience, in the form of summaries of tested strategies, successes and failures, aids iterative refinement on a new task, reducing object-tracking error from 23.35 to 7.77 cm at equal compute and time. We investigate this through a walkthrough of G1 raising a shelf from its side to an upright position. The prior experience comes from four other furniture interactions: carrying a table, lifting a chair, and pulling and pushing a chair.

We first run the four-agent collaborative swarm for 15 minutes and select the best valid motion. From this shared starting solution, we run two separate 15-minute continuations, one with the related experience and one without. Since both receive equal time and compute budgets, this comparison isolates the impact of experience on refinement.

7. Discussion

Swarms trade compute for time

Iterative refinement with simulation feedback improves both object tracking and semantic success over one-shot generation. It allows agents to optimize their proposed motions, inspect the resulting executions and revise their motion and control code. These results support using agentic models for retargeting beyond generating an initial solution. Under a fixed time budget, the collaborative swarm outperforms a single Codex agent (67.5% versus 47.5% semantic success). However, at matched compute, longer single-agent refinement achieves higher semantic success (75% versus 67.5%) at four times the runtime. This suggests that swarms are useful when a solution is needed quickly, while a longer iterative loop remains valuable when more time is available. We encourage the community to study this compute–time tradeoff across more interactions, and to investigate how communication and shared experience can make better use of a limited refinement budget.

Refined references could support RL fine-tuning

Although the reference motions produced by HuMoTAR could be used to train whole-body policies for deployment, our experiments use SONIC without task-specific policy training. A natural next step is to evaluate whether RL fine-tuning on these references helps the robot handle disturbances and changes in the scene. Policy training and hardware deployment remain future work.

Hand morphology limits some interactions

In the example below, G1 must lift and swirl a plate to reproduce the human’s interaction. Although it lifts the plate, it does not complete the swirling motion. The agents adjust finger placement and try two-handed support, yet the grasp remains unstable during rotation. This difficulty reflects the morphological gap between human hands and G1’s Dex3 hands, where the fingers struggle to establish a stable grasp around the plate’s thin rim. Notably, the tested motion and controller revisions do not fully compensate for this mismatch.

Appendix

A1. Can a text description replace a motion reference?

Rather than providing a human motion reference or target object trajectory, we give a single Codex agent a detailed task description, the scene and an initial robot–object state. The agent has 30 minutes to refine an execution that follows the description, using SONIC for the legs and waist and custom arm and hand control. These five examples show the supplied commands and the resulting simulated motions.

Lift and inspect a basket

Text command

A basket rests on the floor in front of the person. Bend toward it and reach down with the right hand to take its handle. Lift the basket clear of the floor while straightening the body. Bring it in front of the torso, then use the other hand to help steady and turn it for inspection. Tilt and rotate the basket so its opening and different sides can be examined, rather than simply holding it motionless. Keep it under control throughout the lift and the changes in orientation. Finish standing with the basket held in front of the body; the demonstrated sequence ends while it is still being held.

Lift, lower and lift a side table

Text command

A small wooden side table with a raised curved rail stands on the floor in front of the person. Bend down, grasp the raised rail and lift the table off the floor as the body straightens. Keep the tabletop approximately level and the legs directed downward. Lower it back onto its legs in front of the body, then pause with the table supported on the floor. Bend toward it again and repeat the lifting action. Make the separate pickup, lowering, supported pause and renewed pickup recognizable, rather than replacing them with a single static hold or dragging the table along the floor. Remain near the table and keep each movement controlled.

Walk and turn while carrying a side table

Text command

Hold the small wooden side table with both hands in front of the torso, keeping its legs clear of the floor. Walk while carrying it along a curved path that turns the body through several facing directions, then comes back toward the starting area. The table travels with the person and turns with the body; its legs remain generally downward and the tabletop remains approximately level, with natural tilting during the turns. Keep both hands supporting the table while stepping and changing direction. Finish the walking sequence still holding the table in front of the body. The interaction should show sustained transport and turning, not stationary lifting or sliding on the floor.

Lift, move and set down a clothes stand

Text command

Stand beside a tall freestanding clothes stand with a circular base and branches near the top. Reach to its vertical pole around waist height with one hand. Lift its base clear of the floor, then take a few steps to move the stand to a nearby position while keeping the pole mostly upright. Move the body together with the stand rather than reaching from a fixed stance. Lower the circular base onto the floor, allow the stand to settle upright, and release the pole. Finish beside the independently standing object with the hand withdrawn. The lift, short transport and deliberate placement should be clearly visible.

Rock a wooden chair and return it upright

Text command

Face a wooden chair and bend toward it, placing both hands around the sides of the seat or nearby frame. Tilt and rock the chair so its seat and back lean and one pair of legs rises while the other pair remains near the floor. Guide the chair back toward an upright orientation and bring all four legs down under control. Keep the hands engaged while the chair changes orientation, and adjust the body with the movement. Finish with the chair upright and supported on its legs. This is a controlled rocking and righting interaction, not carrying the chair away or allowing it to fall.
